The problem

Attention is the scarce resource, not information

A population‑health nurse working a panel between visits can read a chart quickly — trained reviewers clear six to twenty a day. Ninety‑four of these hundred patients have at least one abnormal lab. Reading was never the bottleneck. What each finding costs after you read it is — the call, the order, the chase — and so is deciding which ones earn it.

The product answers one question: of these hundred, which twelve need you this week — and what to do about each. It is a ranked worklist, not a longer list of alerts.

A supervisor briefs three specialists — which records cannot be trusted, who is missing recommended therapy, what was started and never finished — consults all three at once, and orders what they find by what a delay would cost the patient. Every name opens a pre‑visit brief with the evidence attached. About two and a half minutes a run, about a quarter.

Land the one-question sentence and pause on it - that is the product in a line, and everything after it is mechanism. The word that matters is RANKED: the failure mode of every tool in this space is producing more alerts, and a longer list of true things is not help. The three numbers below are why ranking is the job rather than detection - 94 of 100 have an abnormal lab, so finding problems is trivial and choosing between them is not. WHERE "six to twenty a day" COMES FROM, if asked: the 2025 CDI Week Industry Survey, reported in the ACDIS Q&A on Productivity. Most respondents complete 6-10 new patient reviews per day (59.78%), then 11-15 (18.95%); the CDI specialist interviewed runs an expected daily average of 20 including re-reviews. Give the caveat before they do - that is inpatient clinical documentation integrity, a different role and setting from population-health panel work, which carries outreach the CDI number does not. It is cited as evidence that READING is fast, which is the point, not as a throughput target for this user. An earlier version of this slide claimed a nurse reviews about fifteen a week; that was an assumption I could not source, and the argument is stronger without it. The third number is the one to dwell on - it is why data judgment came before any modelling. HOLD THE ARCHITECTURE QUESTION for Q&A rather than spending slide time on it. If asked whether three specialists are worth it, the honest answer is that I A/B-tested my own design against a single agent holding the same tools and it has yet to earn its complexity - same findings, more wall clock, more high-severity findings dropped from the narrative. Offer that; do not bury it. Guardrails on the number: n=3 an arm, the single-agent instruction has had far less tuning, and the canary gap shrinks to 4-of-8 versus 3-of-3 once my own five-run eval is pooled in. The latency gap is the robust part - 267 to 405 seconds against 98 to 162, no overlap. If they push, the answer is that I would ship the single agent and keep the floor, the structured store and the uncited-high check, because the A/B held those constant and neither arm drives uncited findings to zero.
