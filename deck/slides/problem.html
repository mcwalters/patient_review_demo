The problem

Attention is the scarce resource, not information

A population‑health nurse working a panel between visits can read a chart quickly — trained reviewers clear six to twenty a day. Ninety‑four of these hundred patients have at least one abnormal lab. Reading was never the bottleneck. What each finding costs after you read it is — the call, the order, the chase — and so is deciding which ones earn it.

The product answers one question: of these hundred, which twelve need you this week — and what to do about each. It is a ranked worklist, not a longer list of alerts.

A supervisor briefs three specialists — which records cannot be trusted, who is missing recommended therapy, what was started and never finished — consults all three at once, and orders what they find by what a delay would cost the patient. Every name opens a pre‑visit brief with the evidence attached. About two and a half minutes a run, about a quarter.

Land the one-question sentence and pause on it - that is the product in a line, and everything after it is mechanism. The word that matters is RANKED: the failure mode of every tool in this space is producing more alerts, and a longer list of true things is not help. The three numbers below are why ranking is the job rather than detection - 94 of 100 have an abnormal lab, so finding problems is trivial and choosing between them is not. WHERE "six to twenty a day" COMES FROM, if asked: the 2025 CDI Week Industry Survey, reported in the ACDIS Q&A on Productivity. Most respondents complete 6-10 new patient reviews per day (59.78%), then 11-15 (18.95%); the CDI specialist interviewed runs an expected daily average of 20 including re-reviews. Give the caveat before they do - that is inpatient clinical documentation integrity, a different role and setting from population-health panel work, which carries outreach the CDI number does not. It is cited as evidence that READING is fast, which is the point, not as a throughput target for this user. An earlier version of this slide claimed a nurse reviews about fifteen a week; that was an assumption I could not source, and the argument is stronger without it. The third number is the one to dwell on - it is why data judgment came before any modelling. HOLD THE ARCHITECTURE QUESTION for Q&A rather than spending slide time on it. If asked whether three specialists are worth it, the honest answer is that I A/B-tested my own design against a single agent holding the same tools, twice, and the second time it came out even. Six runs an arm: 133s against 136s, ranges fully overlapping, so the latency gap the first battery found was measuring sequential delegation and disappeared when that did. What survives is that the supervisor spends about 2.2 times the model calls for comparable output, and that the single agent's patient set is more reproducible run to run - Jaccard 0.97 against 0.81, which replicated across both batteries. So the split is not paying for itself on anything I can measure, and I would ship the single agent. Two caveats to give rather than have extracted. The counter-evidence is Spearman: the single agent's stable set hides a less stable ORDER, 0.67 against 0.94, and order is what the product sells - but the supervisor's figure rests on one pair, so it cannot carry weight yet. And every measure in that battery is a count or a set-membership check; none reads whether the reasoning is better. The floor, the structured store and the extraction path are held constant, so the most likely reading is that the deterministic scaffolding dominates the topology, which is a more interesting result than either arm winning. The stop-dates canary that the first battery leaned on fully reversed in the second - 0-of-3 against 3-of-3 became 2-of-3 against 0-of-3 - so it is a coin flip and should not be quoted at all. If they push, the answer is that I would ship the single agent and keep the floor, the structured store and the uncited-high check, because the A/B held those constant and neither arm drives uncited findings to zero.
